Tenant emergency at 2am: what landlords should do, step by step

August 27, 2026 · By the RentShepherd team · 9 min read

The short answer

When a tenant calls at 2am: first rule out danger to life — fire, gas smell, or carbon monoxide means 911 or the gas utility before anything else. Then stop the damage: talk the tenant to the water shutoff or breaker panel. Then decide — active water, gas, electrical hazard, no heat in freezing weather, and break-ins justify an immediate dispatch; almost everything else safely waits for morning once the damage is stopped. Finally, document everything and follow up the next day. The rest of this guide walks each step, hazard by hazard.

What actually counts as an emergency

A useful definition: a genuine emergency is a situation that threatens people or actively damages the property — meaning waiting until morning would make someone unsafe or the damage materially worse. Everything else is urgent at most. Here’s the honest sorting:

The five steps, in order

Step 1: Rule out danger to life

Before any landlord logistics: is anyone in danger? A strong gas smell, smoke, or a carbon monoxide alarm means the tenant leaves the unit and calls 911 or the gas utility’s emergency line before calling you — and with gas, they shouldn’t operate light switches or anything that can spark on the way out. Say this plainly in your lease and move-in packet, because at 2am nobody reads documents; they do what they rehearsed.

Step 2: Stop the damage

Most 2am emergencies are water, and water damage is a function of time. The single most valuable thing you can do from bed is talk the tenant to a shutoff: the stop valve behind the toilet or under the sink, the valve on top of the water heater, or the main shutoff. For electrical hazards, it’s the breaker panel — if reaching it is safe. Two minutes of tenant-side mitigation routinely turns a soaked-ceiling claim into a mop-up.

Step 3: Decide — dispatch now, or first thing tomorrow

Once people are safe and the damage is stopped, the decision is financial and practical. After-hours call-outs are billed at a premium, so the question is: with mitigation done, does this still get worse by morning? A burst pipe with the main shut off and no heat in July can usually wait eight hours; an uncontained leak, a freezing unit in a Minneapolis January, or a door that won’t lock cannot. When you do dispatch, send the vendor a complete brief — unit, issue, photos, access notes — so the visit isn’t spent rediscovering what the tenant already told you.

Step 4: Document everything

While it’s happening, not after: when the tenant reported it, what they described, photos or video, what guidance you gave, when you dispatched, and what the vendor found. This record is how insurance claims get paid, how deposit disputes end quickly, and how you spot the third “mystery leak” from the same stack in a year.

Step 5: Follow up the next day

Confirm the fix actually held, check whether secondary damage needs attention (wet drywall, flooring), and close the loop with the tenant. A tenant whose 2am crisis was handled calmly and followed up on is a tenant who renews — the emergency handled well is, quietly, a retention event.

Hazard by hazard: the details that matter

Water

Know — and label — three shutoffs at every property before you need them: fixture stops, the water heater valve, and the main. A leaking water heater usually announces itself as water on the floor; closing its cold-water inlet valve stops the feed. If water is reaching electrical fixtures or the panel, that’s an electrical hazard too — breaker off if safely reachable, and keep the tenant clear.

Gas

Gas gets zero troubleshooting from anyone. Smell means: leave, don’t flip switches, don’t start the car in an attached garage, call the gas utility’s emergency line or 911 from outside. The utility responds around the clock and doesn’t charge to check. Your job as landlord happens afterward: the repair, the paper trail, and making sure detectors are in place where required.

Electrical

Sparking, a burning smell, or a warm outlet plate is an emergency; a single dead outlet usually is not (have the tenant check the GFCI reset first — it resolves a surprising share of “electrical failures”). For the real thing: circuit off at the breaker, nothing plugged back in, electrician with photos already in hand.

No heat, no AC

No heat in freezing weather is an emergency nearly everywhere it freezes — many cold-climate jurisdictions treat heat as an essential service, and beyond the tenant’s welfare, a heatless unit is a pipe-freeze risk to the building itself. Have the tenant check the thermostat batteries and the furnace switch before dispatching; it’s free and occasionally embarrassing in the best way. Extreme heat is the mirror image: in a Phoenix or Las Vegas summer, a dead AC with elderly tenants or young children in the unit deserves emergency treatment, whatever the statute says.

Security

If a crime occurred, the police report comes first — then your job is making the unit securable tonight: a board-up, a hasp and padlock, a same-night lock change, whatever gets the tenant safely behind a locking door. The permanent repair is a morning problem; the securable door is not.

Prepare before the phone rings

  • Label the shutoffs — water main, water heater valve, breaker panel — and photograph their locations into the tenant’s move-in packet.
  • Define “emergency” in the lease, with the 911-first rule for danger to life.
  • Line up vendors now: a plumber, an electrician, and an HVAC tech who take after-hours calls — plus a backup each. The 2am scramble is for finding your phone, not finding a plumber.
  • Decide your dispatch authority in advance — what you’ll spend without deliberating at 2am. A pre-made decision is the difference between a groggy approval and an hour of doom-scrolling plumber reviews.
  • Have a front line. The worst version of every scenario above starts with “the tenant couldn’t reach anyone.” An always-on answering layer — human or AI — means the report is heard, triaged, and acted on in seconds, not discovered in voicemail at 8am.

FAQ: rental emergencies

Is no air conditioning an emergency?

It depends on conditions and jurisdiction. In extreme heat, a dead AC can be genuinely dangerous — especially for elderly tenants, young children, or anyone with health conditions — and some jurisdictions treat cooling as an essential service. In mild weather it’s urgent, not a 2am dispatch. Judge by the heat and the tenant, not the appliance.

Should tenants call 911 before calling me?

Yes — whenever there’s danger to life: fire, a strong gas smell, a CO alarm, a medical emergency, or a crime in progress. You are the second call. Put that in the lease and the move-in packet so nobody hesitates at 2am.

Do I have to pay after-hours emergency rates?

For a genuine emergency, usually yes — and the premium is still cheaper than a night of active water damage. The savings come from triage (knowing what truly waits until morning) and from tenant-side mitigation like a closed shutoff valve, which can turn an emergency dispatch into a next-day appointment.

What if I just can’t be reached?

Design that scenario away before it happens: 911-first instructions for danger to life, labeled shutoffs at move-in, and an always-on front line — an answering service or an AI property manager — so an emergency report is never sitting unread until morning.
